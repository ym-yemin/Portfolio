Governments use a range of mechanisms to encourage renewable-energy investment. These include renewable portfolio standards, certificates, auctions, tax incentives, subsidies and feed-in tariffs.

Among these approaches, the feed-in tariff has historically played an important role in the early growth of wind-energy markets. A well-designed tariff can reduce investment uncertainty by establishing predictable revenue for a project over a defined period.

## What makes a feed-in tariff effective?

Three elements are especially important: an appropriate payment level, a sufficiently long purchase agreement and reliable access to the electricity grid.

Tariff design must balance two competing goals. Payments should be high enough to make renewable projects investable, but they should not generate excessive returns or encourage inefficient development.

## Fixed and premium models

Feed-in mechanisms can broadly be separated into fixed-price and market-related premium systems. A fixed tariff gives generators predictable revenue, while premium models expose projects to a greater degree of electricity-market price risk.

Tariff degression can also be used. Under this approach, support for new installations gradually declines as the technology matures and costs fall. Done carefully, this can encourage innovation while protecting investors from sudden policy changes.

## Different markets need different designs

Wind resources, project sizes, financing conditions and electricity-market structures vary widely between countries. This means there is no single tariff design that works everywhere.

Germany, France, China and Thailand provide useful examples of how feed-in policies have been adapted to local circumstances. Designs have included resource-based tariffs, long-term fixed prices, premiums and differentiation between regions or project characteristics.

## Policy should evolve with technology

As wind technology becomes more competitive, policy design needs to evolve as well. Support should be predictable enough to reduce investment risk while remaining flexible enough to respond to falling technology costs and changing market conditions.

The central lesson is that policy effectiveness depends less on simply offering a high subsidy and more on creating a stable, transparent and well-designed investment framework.
